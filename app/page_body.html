The 50M parameter cap counts token embeddings. That turns vocabulary size from a preprocessing detail into a capacity allocation decision: with GPT‑2’s 50,257-token vocabulary, 54% of the entire budget is a lookup table, leaving room for just 7 transformer layers. An 8k vocabulary buys 14 layers for the same parameters. So we measured which is actually better — and the answer splits in two.

Depth buys compression; vocabulary buys reasoning — and at a fixed 50M budget they pull against each other. Neither extreme wins. In our sweep the best model spends about a third of the budget on embeddings, keeping 84% of the best compression while topping the reasoning score. The lookup table is not waste, and it is not free.

The measured tradeoff

Shrinking the vocabulary is not free: fewer, coarser tokens mean more tokens per byte of text, which costs compute and shortens effective context. So the study fixes both the parameter budget and the FLOP budget, and asks which allocation wins. Bits‑per‑byte barely moves. Reasoning accuracy moves enormously — in the other direction.

The two curves disagree, and the optimum is interior. Compression is best at a 2,048-token vocabulary; reasoning is best at 8,192. Pushed to the extreme — 16,384 tokens, 73.5% of the budget in embeddings, only 4 layers left — compression collapses by 12.5% for no further reasoning gain. Our hypothesis was that the smallest vocabulary would win on both. It lost on one and the experiment corrected us.

Why bits‑per‑byte, not perplexity. Per‑token perplexity is not comparable across tokenizers: a larger vocabulary makes each token carry more text, so it predicts fewer and harder tokens and its perplexity looks better without the model modelling the underlying text any better. Normalising by the raw bytes of held‑out text removes the tokenizer from the metric entirely. Every comparison here is in bits‑per‑byte; token perplexity is shown only to demonstrate how badly it misleads.

Two reasoning columns, because the obvious one is confounded. Free generation (“gen”) rewards large vocabularies for a reason that has nothing to do with reasoning: the gold answer is fewer tokens, so greedy decoding has fewer chances to slip. Rescoring every candidate by byte‑normalised likelihood — how ARC and HellaSwag are scored — shrinks the apparent effect by 1.6×. Both are reported; the forced‑choice column is the one to trust.

Scale caveat. This pilot is ~2.9M parameters on a synthetic corpus, and with 80 held‑out items the reasoning confidence intervals are roughly ±11 points — the 65% and 50% rows overlap. The trend is suggestive, not settled. The flagship 50M sweep on TinyStories and FineWeb‑Edu is the load‑bearing evidence.
